Do Markets Know Inflation First?

Every month, real money trades on Kalshi contracts like “Will CPI rise more than 0.3%?”. Differencing those prices gives a full market-implied probability distribution over the upcoming CPI print. This page lets you explore five years of them and compare the market’s forecast with a professional model.

How it works

1 · Prices become probabilities

Each monthly event lists 5–15 threshold contracts spaced 0.1 pp apart. The yes-price of “CPI > x” is the market’s probability that the print exceeds x, so differencing adjacent prices slices the whole distribution into outcomes. The bins telescope to exactly 1; the only incoherence a threshold market can show is a higher threshold priced above a lower one, which is repaired with a running minimum and recorded.

2 · Rules decided before results

Price is the end-of-day bid/ask midpoint, never a stale last trade. Contracts quoted 0/100 are dropped. Ground truth is the first-release BLS number the market settled on. Every rule was written down before the evaluation ran, and every raw API response is committed untouched.

3 · A fair comparison

The Cleveland Fed’s daily nowcast is read on the same date as the market, 30, 14, 7 and 1 days before each release; naive baselines only use prints already published. The Diebold–Mariano test asks whether one forecaster’s errors are genuinely smaller month after month. October 2025 is excluded from the headline sample because the BLS never published it.

Limitations

  • 56 monthly releases, dominated by the 2021–23 inflation spike.
  • Thin liquidity early in each market’s life; spread and volume are shown for every day so you can judge.
  • The open-ended tail bins are valued one grid step beyond the last threshold, an assumption that matters most in high-inflation months.
  • No economist-consensus benchmark: no free archive exists.
